\chapter{Biophysics}
\label{ch:bio}

\buildson{statistical mechanics (Chapter~\ref{ch:statmech}), fluid dynamics (Chapter~\ref{ch:fluids})}

\chapterdek{Physics turned loose on the machinery of living things: motors that walk, cables that fire, molecules that fold, and why a bacterium cannot coast.}

\begin{prototype}
The random walk. Inside a cell, every molecule is battered by water molecules trillions of times a second and wanders aimlessly. Life does not fight this chaos; it exploits it. Diffusion delivers its supplies, thermal fluctuations fold its proteins, and its motors ratchet random kicks into directed motion.
\end{prototype}

Living things obey the laws of physics, and nothing else. But they inhabit a corner of the physical world with unusual properties: everything is wet, warm, small, and crowded; energies are comparable to thermal energy; inertia is irrelevant; and systems are driven far from equilibrium by a constant supply of chemical fuel. Biophysics asks how the physics of this world constrains and enables life. Schr\"odinger asked ``What is life?'' in 1944 and helped inspire the discovery of DNA's structure, made by a physicist and a biologist using the physics of X-ray diffraction. The traffic between the fields has never stopped.

\section{The world of the cell}

Begin with the numbers. A bacterium is about $1\un{\mu m}$ long; a human cell about $20\un{\mu m}$; a protein a few nanometres. The forces that matter are piconewtons. And the unit of energy is thermal energy at body temperature:
\[
  \kB T\approx4.3\times10^{-21}\un{J}\approx4.3\un{pN\,nm}\approx\tfrac{1}{37}\un{eV}.
\]
A force of a few piconewtons acting over a few nanometres does work of order $\kB T$. That is no coincidence: molecular machines must operate just above the thermal noise, strong enough to work reliably but weak enough to be built from floppy molecules and switched by single chemical reactions. The cell's fuel, the hydrolysis of one ATP molecule, releases about $20\kB T$.

Two more facts shape everything. First, cells live at absurdly low Reynolds number, around $10^{-5}$ (Chapter~\ref{ch:fluids}): water is to a bacterium as honey is to you, and inertia is meaningless. Second, cells are crowded, about $30\%$ protein by volume, more like a gel than a dilute solution.

\section{Diffusion}

A molecule in water performs a random walk, spreading so that $\langle x^2\rangle = 2Dt$ in each direction (Chapter~\ref{ch:statmech}). For a typical protein, $D\approx10\un{\mu m^2/s}$. The time to diffuse a distance $L$ is
\[
  t\sim\frac{L^2}{D}.
\]
Across a bacterium ($1\un{\mu m}$): a tenth of a second. Across a human cell ($20\un{\mu m}$): about a minute. Down a nerve axon a metre long: years. Diffusion is fast over short distances and hopelessly slow over long ones. This single scaling law explains why cells are small, why large cells have internal transport systems, why animals need circulatory systems, and why your lungs branch into $300$ million tiny alveoli.

\section{Molecular machines}

Cells are full of machines that turn chemical energy into motion.
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=3pt]
  \item \textbf{Kinesin} walks along microtubule tracks, carrying cargo from the cell body down long axons. It takes $8\un{nm}$ steps, one per ATP, against loads up to about $6\un{pN}$, ``hand over hand'' with two heads. It has been watched taking single steps in optical traps.
  \item \textbf{Myosin} pulls on actin filaments; billions of them acting together contract your muscles.
  \item \textbf{ATP synthase} is a rotary motor. Protons flowing through it across a membrane turn a rotor, and the rotation forces ADP and phosphate together into ATP. Run backwards, it spins by burning ATP. It is nearly $100\%$ efficient.
  \item \textbf{The bacterial flagellar motor}, about $45\un{nm}$ across, spins a corkscrew flagellum at hundreds of revolutions per second, driven by a flow of ions, and can reverse direction.
\end{itemize}

How can a motor work when it is constantly kicked about by thermal noise many times larger than its step? One answer is a \emph{Brownian ratchet}: let thermal fluctuations do the moving, and use chemical energy only to rectify them --- to prevent backward steps. Feynman famously analysed a ratchet and pawl and showed that in a single heat bath it cannot extract work, or it would violate the second law (Chapter~\ref{ch:thermo}). Molecular motors evade this only because they are driven out of equilibrium by burning ATP (Problem~\ref{prob:ratchet}).

\section{Polymers}

DNA and proteins are \emph{polymers}: long chains of repeating units. A flexible polymer of $N$ segments of length $b$ adopts a random-walk shape, with end-to-end distance
\[
  R\approx\sqrt N\,b,
\]
much smaller than its contour length $Nb$ (Chapter~\ref{ch:math}). Real polymers resist bending over a \emph{persistence length}; for DNA, about $50\un{nm}$, so a short piece is a stiff rod and a long one a random coil.

Pulling the ends of a polymer apart reduces the number of available configurations, i.e.\ its entropy. The chain therefore resists with a force that is purely entropic, like a gas resisting compression:
\[
  F = \frac{3\kB T}{Nb^2}\,x .
\]
This \emph{entropic spring} is stiffer at higher temperature, which is why a rubber band contracts when heated (Chapter~\ref{ch:thermo}). Single DNA molecules have been stretched with optical tweezers and magnetic tweezers, and their force--extension curves match polymer theory beautifully.

Packaging is a feat. The DNA in a single human cell is about $2\un{m}$ long, yet it fits in a nucleus $6\un{\mu m}$ across, wound around protein spools, coiled, and folded --- while remaining accessible to the machines that read it.

\section{Electricity in cells}

A cell membrane is a lipid bilayer $5\un{nm}$ thick, an excellent insulator. Pumps in the membrane, burning ATP, maintain concentration differences: potassium is concentrated inside, sodium outside. When the membrane is permeable to one ion, that ion diffuses down its concentration gradient until the resulting voltage balances the diffusion. Equilibrium requires the Boltzmann factor $e^{-qV/\kB T}$ to account for the concentration ratio, giving the \textbf{Nernst potential}
\[
  V = \frac{\kB T}{q}\ln\frac{c_{\text{out}}}{c_{\text{in}}}.
\]
At body temperature, $\kB T/e\approx27\un{mV}$, and a tenfold concentration ratio gives about $60\un{mV}$. A resting neuron sits at about $-70\un{mV}$, near the potassium Nernst potential. The field across the thin membrane is about $10^7\un{V/m}$, near the breakdown strength of most insulators.

A neuron signals with an \textbf{action potential}. A small depolarization opens voltage-gated sodium channels; sodium rushes in, depolarizing the membrane further and opening more channels: positive feedback. Within a millisecond the voltage swings to about $+40\un{mV}$; then the sodium channels close and potassium channels open, restoring the resting state. The spike propagates along the axon as a nonlinear travelling wave, regenerated at every point. Hodgkin and Huxley measured the currents in the giant axon of the squid and wrote down, in 1952, a set of nonlinear differential equations that predicted the shape and speed of the spike --- one of the triumphs of quantitative biology. In vertebrates, insulating \emph{myelin} sheaths force the signal to jump between gaps, speeding conduction to about $100\un{m/s}$.

\section{Protein folding}

A protein is a chain of a hundred or more amino acids that folds, in seconds or less, into a precise three-dimensional shape that determines its function. In 1969 Cyrus Levinthal pointed out that if each amino acid could take just three orientations, a chain of $100$ would have $3^{100}\approx10^{48}$ conformations; sampling them at one per picosecond would take far longer than the age of the universe. Yet proteins fold. The \emph{Levinthal paradox} is resolved by the shape of the energy landscape: it is a rugged \emph{funnel}, sloping downhill toward the native state, so the chain is guided rather than searching at random. A major driving force is the \emph{hydrophobic effect}: oily amino acids cluster in the core because water molecules around them are forced into ordered cages, so burying them \emph{increases} the entropy of the water. Misfolded proteins cause diseases from Alzheimer's to prion diseases. Predicting structure from sequence was a fifty-year grand challenge until the machine-learning system AlphaFold largely solved it in 2020.

\section{The physics of the senses}

Our senses operate at the limits physics allows. A dark-adapted rod cell in your retina responds to a \emph{single photon}: absorbing one photon isomerizes one retinal molecule, triggering a cascade that amplifies it a millionfold. In dim light, what you see is limited by the Poisson statistics of photon arrivals. The ear detects vibrations of the eardrum smaller than the diameter of an atom, near the limit set by thermal noise. The cochlea is a mechanical Fourier analyser (Chapter~\ref{ch:math}): a tapered membrane resonates at different frequencies at different points along its length, and hair cells actively amplify the vibrations, so actively that healthy ears emit faint sounds of their own. Some birds appear to sense the Earth's magnetic field through a quantum effect in light-activated molecules in their eyes --- a leading example of the emerging field of quantum biology.

\section{Scaling laws}

Across all mammals, from shrews to whales, the metabolic rate scales with body mass as
\[
  P\propto M^{3/4},
\]
\emph{Kleiber's law} (1932). A pure surface-to-volume argument would give $M^{2/3}$ (heat is lost through the skin); the observed $3/4$ is often explained by the geometry of branching supply networks like blood vessels. Many other quantities follow quarter-power laws. Lifespan grows as about $M^{1/4}$ and heart rate falls as $M^{-1/4}$, so the number of heartbeats in a lifetime is roughly the same, about a billion, for a mouse and an elephant. Small animals live fast: a shrew must eat its own weight in food every day.

\section{Information}

A cell is an information-processing machine. The human genome holds about $3\times10^{9}$ base pairs, each worth two bits: about $750$ megabytes, less than a film. Copying it requires extraordinary accuracy, about one error per $10^9$ bases, yet the free energy differences between correct and incorrect base pairing would by themselves allow only about one error in $10^4$. In 1974 John Hopfield showed how cells beat this limit, by \emph{kinetic proofreading}: adding an irreversible, energy-consuming checking step multiplies the selectivity (Problem~\ref{prob:proofreading}). Accuracy costs energy --- another case of information being physical (Chapter~\ref{ch:thermo}).

\section{Tools}

Biophysics is also a toolkit. X-ray crystallography revealed the double helix and the structures of hundreds of thousands of proteins (Chapter~\ref{ch:condmat}). \emph{Cryo-electron microscopy} images flash-frozen molecules at near-atomic resolution (Nobel Prize, 2017). Fluorescence microscopy lights up specific molecules in living cells, and \emph{super-resolution} methods beat the diffraction limit of Chapter~\ref{ch:optics} by switching molecules on a few at a time and locating each precisely (Nobel Prize, 2014). \emph{Optical tweezers} grip single molecules and measure their piconewton forces (Nobel Prize, 2018; Chapter~\ref{ch:amo}).

\section{Problems}

\begin{problems}
\prob{1}\core Express $\kB T$ at $37^\circ\text{C}$ in joules, pN\,nm, and meV. Kinesin takes $8\un{nm}$ steps against up to $6\un{pN}$, using one ATP ($\approx20\kB T$) per step. What is its maximum efficiency?

\prob{1}\core A protein has $D = 10\un{\mu m^2/s}$. How long does it take to diffuse across a bacterium ($1\un{\mu m}$), a human cell ($20\un{\mu m}$), and a $1\un{m}$ axon? Kinesin moves at about $1\un{\mu m/s}$. At what distance does active transport beat diffusion?

\prob{1} A human cell holds $6\times10^9$ base pairs of DNA, each $0.34\un{nm}$ long. Find the total length and compare it with the diameter of the nucleus, $6\un{\mu m}$. If the DNA were a random coil with persistence length $50\un{nm}$ (segment length $b = 100\un{nm}$), how big would the coil be?

\prob{2}\core Inside a neuron the potassium concentration is $140\un{mM}$; outside it is $5\un{mM}$. Find the potassium Nernst potential at $37^\circ\text{C}$. For sodium, $12\un{mM}$ inside and $145\un{mM}$ outside. Explain why opening sodium channels makes the inside positive.

\prob{2}\core \emph{(The entropic spring.)} For a random-walk polymer, the end-to-end vector $\vb R$ has a Gaussian distribution, $P(\vb R)\propto e^{-3R^2/2Nb^2}$. Using $S = \kB\ln P + \text{const}$ and $F = -T\,\partial S/\partial R$, derive the entropic spring force. Why does the spring get stiffer as temperature increases?

\prob{2} \emph{(A bacterium cannot coast.)} \emph{E.~coli} is about $1\un{\mu m}$ in size, has mass about $10^{-15}\un{kg}$, and swims at $30\un{\mu m/s}$ in water. If its motor suddenly stops, how far does it coast? Use Stokes drag, $6\pi\eta rv$, to find the stopping time. Compare the distance with the size of a hydrogen atom.

\prob{2} Make Levinthal's estimate for a $100$-residue protein with $3$ conformations per residue, sampled at $10^{12}$ per second. How long would a random search take? Real proteins fold in microseconds to seconds. By what factor is the search space effectively reduced?

\prob{2} Using Kleiber's law, by what factor is the metabolic rate per kilogram of a $20\un{g}$ mouse greater than that of a $5000\un{kg}$ elephant? If both live about a billion heartbeats, and a mouse's heart beats $600$ times per minute, estimate the lifespans of both.

\prob{2} In an unmyelinated axon, the signal speed scales as the square root of the axon's diameter; in a myelinated one, linearly with diameter. The squid evolved a giant axon $0.5\un{mm}$ across to escape predators quickly. Why did vertebrates not need giant axons? How much thinner can a myelinated axon be for the same speed?

\prob{3}\core \emph{(The physical limit to smelling.)} A bacterium measures the concentration $c$ of an attractant with a receptor of size $a$, over a time $T$. Argue that the number of molecules it counts is about $Dac\,T$ (the rate at which molecules diffuse into a sphere of radius $a$ is $\sim Dac$), and that counting statistics give a fractional error $\delta c/c\sim1/\sqrt{DacT}$. This is the \emph{Berg--Purcell limit}. Estimate it for $c = 1\un{\mu M}$, $a = 1\un{\mu m}$, $T=1\un{s}$. Real bacteria come within a factor of a few of it.

\prob{3} \label{prob:ratchet} \emph{(Feynman's ratchet.)} A ratchet and pawl attached to paddles in a gas would seem to rectify random molecular kicks into one-way rotation, lifting a weight. Explain why it fails if the pawl is at the same temperature as the gas: the pawl itself jiggles and sometimes lets the ratchet slip back, at exactly the rate needed to balance forward steps. Show that the device works as an engine only if the paddles and pawl are at different temperatures, and that its efficiency is then at most Carnot's. How does a kinesin motor get around the problem?

\prob{3} \label{prob:proofreading} \emph{(Kinetic proofreading.)} An enzyme must pick the right substrate over a wrong one that binds with free energy weaker by $\Delta G$. In a single equilibrium binding step, the error rate is about $e^{-\Delta G/\kB T}$. Hopfield proposed that after binding, the complex is converted (using energy) into an activated form, from which either the product is made or the substrate falls off. Argue that if falling off happens at the same rate ratio as the original unbinding, the error rate becomes $e^{-2\Delta G/\kB T}$. Why must the activation step consume energy to achieve this?
\end{problems}
